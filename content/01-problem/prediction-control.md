## Textbook

### Two problems {#two}

Almost every method in reinforcement learning solves one of two problems.

- **Prediction**, also called policy evaluation: given a policy $\pol{\pi}$, estimate its value function $\val{v_\pi}$ or $\val{q_\pi}$. The policy is fixed; the question is how good it is.
- **Control**: find an optimal policy $\pol{\pi_*}$, or at least a good one. The policy changes; the question is what to do.

Prediction is a part of control. A control method must judge its current policy before it can improve it, and in practice every control method contains a prediction method at its core: [[sarsa]] is TD(0) prediction for action values, plus a policy that follows the estimates.

### From prediction to control {#gpi}

The bridge between the two is **policy improvement**. Given the action values of a policy, a better policy is the one that, in every state, picks an action with the highest value. The policy improvement theorem makes this precise ([[policy-improvement]]).

::: theorem {#thm-improvement} Policy improvement (Sutton & Barto, §4.2)
Let $\pol{\pi}$ and $\pol{\pi'}$ be deterministic policies such that $\val{q_\pi(s, \pi'(s))} \ge \val{v_\pi(s)}$ for every state $s$. Then $\val{v_{\pi'}(s)} \ge \val{v_\pi(s)}$ for every state: $\pol{\pi'}$ is at least as good as $\pol{\pi}$.
:::

The greedy policy with respect to $\val{q_\pi}$ satisfies the condition, because $\max_a \val{q_\pi(s,a)} \ge \sum_a \pol{\pi(a \mid s)}\,\val{q_\pi(s,a)} = \val{v_\pi(s)}$. So evaluation followed by greedy improvement never makes a policy worse, and if it does not make it better, the policy already satisfies the Bellman optimality equation and is optimal ([[optimality]]).

Alternating the two steps is **policy iteration** ([[policy-iteration]]). Most control methods do a looser version of it: they improve the policy before the evaluation is finished, sometimes after a single step. Sutton and Barto call this whole family **generalized policy iteration** ([[gpi]]): two processes, one making the values consistent with the policy and one making the policy greedy with respect to the values, which pull against each other in the short run and meet at the optimum.

### On-policy and off-policy {#on-off}

Both problems come in two variants, depending on where the experience comes from ([[on-off-policy]]).

- **On-policy**: the policy being evaluated or improved is the one generating the data. TD(0) and SARSA are on-policy.
- **Off-policy**: the data come from a different, *behavior* policy, often one that explores more. Estimating $\val{v_\pi}$ from data generated by another policy needs a correction for the difference between the two ([[importance-sampling]]); Q-learning learns the greedy policy's values without one ([[q-learning]]).

Policy-gradient methods are the exception in form, since they adjust the policy directly by gradient ascent, but those with a critic still contain a prediction method.

### Prediction for its own sake {#why-prediction}

Prediction is useful on its own, not only as a step toward control. A game program may need the probability of winning from a position against a fixed opponent; an engineer may want the expected energy cost of a fixed controller before deploying it. And in actor–critic methods ([[actor-critic]]) the critic solves a prediction problem, the evaluation of the actor's current policy, while the actor solves control.

## Card

### Idea

**Prediction**: the policy is given; how good is it? Estimate $\val{v_\pi}$ or $\val{q_\pi}$. **Control**: find a better policy, ideally an optimal one. Control is prediction plus improvement, repeated: evaluate, act more greedily, evaluate again.

::: analogy
Prediction is a coach rating the current game plan. Control is the coach changing the plan, rating the new one, and changing it again.
:::

### In symbols {#formula}

$$\text{prediction: } \pol{\pi} \mapsto \val{v_\pi},\ \val{q_\pi} \qquad\qquad \text{control: find } \pol{\pi_*}$$

$$\text{improvement: } \pol{\pi'(s)} = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_a \val{q_\pi(s,a)}$$

### Why it matters {#why}

Knowing which of the two problems a method solves explains half of its design. The label on every algorithm in the atlas says which one it is.

### Pitfalls

- Judging a control method by its values while it is still exploring: its values describe the exploring policy, not the final one.
- Expecting a prediction method to change the policy: it only measures it.

### Check yourself {#check}

::: question
Is TD(0) a prediction method or a control method? And SARSA?
---
TD(0) is prediction: it estimates $\val{v_\pi}$ for a fixed policy. SARSA is control: it estimates action values and acts ε-greedily on them, so its policy keeps improving.
:::

::: question
Why does acting greedily with respect to $\val{q_\pi}$ never make a policy worse?
---
In every state the greedy action is worth at least the policy's average, $\val{v_\pi(s)}$. By the policy improvement theorem, the new policy is at least as good everywhere.
:::
