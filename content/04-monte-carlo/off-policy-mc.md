## Story

::: step {run = "off", at = 0, tiles = "policy", formula = 1}
**The player we learn from plays at random**: in every situation it tosses a coin to hit or stick. The policy we want is a different one: the greedy policy, which plays the move with the best value. Can we learn the best play by watching nothing but coin tosses? Yes, if each return is weighted by how likely the greedy policy was to make the same moves.
:::

::: step {run = "off", at = 0, play = 1, pace = 700, formula = 2}
First hand: 15 against a 4. The coin says hit, a 7 comes, bust: $\rew{-1}$. Walking back from the end, the last move is updated with weight $W = 1$. Now the greedy policy prefers to stick at 15, so the coin's move is not the one it would have made, and the walk stops there: anything earlier in the hand would describe a different policy.
:::

::: step {run = "off", at = 2, play = 1, pace = 700, formula = 2}
Third hand: 20 against a 9. The coin says hit, a terrible move, but an ace comes: 21. Stick, the dealer busts, $\rew{+1}$. Walking back: sticking on 21 is what the greedy policy would do, so the walk goes on, and the weight doubles to $W = 2$, one over the coin's probability of $\tfrac12$. Hitting on 20 now looks like a winner: one lucky hand can mislead the estimates until more hands come in.
:::

::: step {run = "off", at = 1000, tiles = "policy"}
After 1000 random hands the greedy strategy is still rough: fewer than half the situations get the optimal move. Most hands are cut short: the longer a random hand, the more likely the coin disagreed with the greedy policy somewhere.
:::

::: step {run = "off", at = 100000, tiles = "policy"}
After 100,000 random hands the greedy strategy is close to the optimal one, although the player we watched never played it. That is **off-policy** learning: learning about one policy from the experience of another.
:::

::: step {run = "off", at = 100000, tiles = "policy", curves = ["chart", "es"], metric = "match"}
Averaged over 10 runs, learning from random play is slower than Monte Carlo ES, which plays its own best guess: only the ends of hands that agree with the greedy policy teach anything. [Compare them in the Lab](lab:blackjack-control).
:::

## Textbook

### Two policies {#two}

Control has a built-in tension. The values worth learning are those of the best behavior, which never wastes a move, yet the only way to find that behavior is to waste moves on exploration ([[explore-exploit]]). On-policy control settles for a compromise: it learns the values of a policy that keeps exploring, a little worse than the best ([[mc-control]]). Off-policy control gives the two roles to two policies. A **behavior policy** $\pol{b}$ does the exploring and generates the episodes. A **target policy** $\pol{\pi}$ is the one whose values are learned, and it can be the greedy, eventually optimal policy, because it never has to act. The data come from off the target policy, hence the name **off-policy** learning ([[on-off-policy]]).

Episodes from $\pol{b}$ can only teach about actions that $\pol{b}$ actually takes. Whatever $\pol{\pi}$ might do, $\pol{b}$ must therefore sometimes do too: $\pol{\pi(a \mid s)} > 0$ requires $\pol{b(a \mid s)} > 0$. This is the **coverage** assumption. It forces the behavior to be random wherever it disagrees with the target, while the target itself may be deterministic, as the greedy policy of control is.

### Importance sampling {#is}

Returns observed under $\pol{b}$ have the wrong expectation: $\mathbb{E}_b[\rew{G_t} \mid S_t = s] = \val{v_b(s)}$. They are corrected by **importance sampling**, weighting each return by the relative probability of its trajectory under the two policies ([[importance-sampling]]). Given a start state $S_t$, the probability of the rest of the trajectory under a policy is a product of policy probabilities and transition probabilities, and the transition probabilities cancel in the ratio:

$$\rho_{t:T-1} = \frac{\prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \pol{\pi(A_k \mid S_k)}\, p(S_{k+1} \mid S_k, A_k)}{\prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \pol{b(A_k \mid S_k)}\, p(S_{k+1} \mid S_k, A_k)} = \prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \frac{\pol{\pi(A_k \mid S_k)}}{\pol{b(A_k \mid S_k)}}. \label{ratio}$$

The ratio depends only on the two policies and the sequence, not on the MDP, so it can be computed without a model, and $\mathbb{E}_b[\rho_{t:T-1}\,\rew{G_t} \mid S_t = s] = \val{v_\pi(s)}$. For action values the first action is given, so the ratio starts one step later, at $t + 1$.

Averaging the weighted returns directly is **ordinary** importance sampling. Dividing instead by the sum of the weights is **weighted** importance sampling, which is biased but far less variable, and is the usual choice ([[importance-sampling]]). With weights $W_k$, the weighted average of returns $G_k$ can be kept incrementally with a running sum of the weights $C$:

$$\val{V} \leftarrow \val{V} + \frac{W}{C}\,\big[\,\rew{G} - \val{V}\,\big], \qquad C \leftarrow C + W \text{ (before the update)}. \label{weighted}$$

### Off-policy Monte Carlo control {#control}

Off-policy Monte Carlo control follows the behavior policy while learning about and improving the target policy. Let the target policy be greedy with respect to $\val{Q}$, a deterministic policy, so that $\pol{\pi(A_k \mid S_k)}$ is 1 if $A_k$ is the greedy action and 0 otherwise. Walking backward through an episode, the weight of each return is then the product of $1 / \pol{b(A_k \mid S_k)}$ over the later actions, as long as they were all greedy. The first time the behavior took a non-greedy action, the weight of every earlier step is 0, and the backward walk can stop.

::: algorithm {#alg-off} Off-policy Monte Carlo control, for estimating $\pol{\pi} \approx \pol{\pi_*}$
For every state and action: any value $\val{Q(s,a)}$, and $C(s,a) = 0$; $\pol{\pi(s)}$ is a greedy action for $\val{Q}$, ties broken by a fixed rule
Repeat for each episode:
  Pick a soft behavior policy $\pol{b}$, and play one episode with it: $S_0, A_0, \rew{R_1}, \ldots, S_{T-1}, A_{T-1}, \rew{R_T}$
  $\rew{G} \leftarrow 0$; $W \leftarrow 1$
  For $t = T-1$ down to $0$:
    $\rew{G} \leftarrow \gam\,\rew{G} + \rew{R_{t+1}}$
    $C(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow C(S_t, A_t) + W$
    $\val{Q(S_t, A_t)} \leftarrow \val{Q(S_t, A_t)} + \frac{W}{C(S_t, A_t)}\,[\rew{G} - \val{Q(S_t, A_t)}]$
    $\pol{\pi(S_t)} \leftarrow$ a greedy action for $\val{Q(S_t, \cdot)}$, by the same tie rule
    If $A_t \ne \pol{\pi(S_t)}$: stop this episode's walk, since every earlier step would get weight 0
    $W \leftarrow W / \pol{b(A_t \mid S_t)}$
:::

The return of the last step is always used with weight 1, because a pair's own action carries no ratio. Ties in the arg max must be broken consistently, otherwise the test $A_t \ne \pol{\pi(S_t)}$ is meaningless. The behavior policy can be anything soft: uniformly random, or ε-greedy with respect to the current estimates, which keeps more of each episode.

::: remark
For a fixed target policy, the weighted estimates are consistent: as the sum of the weights $C(s,a)$ grows, the weighted average converges to $\val{q_\pi(s,a)}$, provided the behavior keeps covering every pair. With the target policy changing as the estimates change, the argument is the one of generalized policy iteration ([[gpi]]): the method cannot settle on a suboptimal policy, because that policy's values would make greedy improvement change it. As for Monte Carlo ES, a general convergence proof for this exact algorithm is not standard.
:::

### The cost: learning from tails {#tails}

Every update in the backward walk needs all the later actions to agree with the greedy target, so the method learns only from the **tails** of episodes: the stretch after the behavior's last non-greedy move. With a random behavior that stretch is short. With two actions chosen at random, a $k$-step tail agrees with a deterministic target with probability $2^{-k}$, so states early in long episodes are almost never updated. Blackjack hands are short, so the method works there; in tasks with long episodes it can be greatly slowed down. Temporal-difference methods address this by needing no ratio at all for one-step targets ([[q-learning]]), and per-decision importance sampling and eligibility traces reduce the problem for multi-step returns (Precup, Sutton & Singh, 2000).

### Example: learning Blackjack from random play {#example}

With a behavior policy that hits or sticks at random in every state, off-policy Monte Carlo learns a greedy strategy close to the optimal one ([[exploring-starts]]). \ref{fig-match} shows how fast, compared with Monte Carlo ES, which plays its own current best guess after a random start: the share of states whose greedy action is optimal grows more slowly when learning from random play, because most of each random hand is cut off, and early decisions of long hands are rarely learned from.

### Properties {#properties}

- **Learns the optimal policy while behaving otherwise.** The behavior can explore freely; the target policy is greedy.
- **Can learn from data it did not choose:** recorded episodes of another agent, a human, or an older version of itself, as long as the behavior's probabilities are known.
- **Learns only from tails.** Everything before the last non-greedy action is discarded, which can make learning very slow.
- **High variance.** Weights are products of ratios and can be large; weighted importance sampling tames them, at the price of some bias.
- **Episodic only,** like all Monte Carlo methods.

### Historical remarks {#history}

Importance sampling is a classical technique of Monte Carlo simulation (Kahn & Marshall, 1953; Rubinstein, 1981). Its use for off-policy learning in reinforcement learning was developed by Precup, Sutton and Singh (2000), among others. The off-policy Monte Carlo control algorithm, with weighted importance sampling and its incremental implementation, follows Sutton and Barto (§5.6–5.7).

## Card

### Idea

Behave one way, learn about another. Episodes come from an exploratory behavior policy; the values learned are those of the greedy policy. Each return is weighted by how much more (or less) likely the greedy policy was to produce the same moves; at the first move the greedy policy would not have made, stop.

::: analogy
Learning the best route through a city by studying the trips of a tourist who turns at random. Only the last part of each trip, from the last wrong turn on, is a trip the best driver could also have made, and those parts are what you learn from.
:::

### The update {#update}

$$C(S_t,A_t) \leftarrow C(S_t,A_t) + W \qquad \val{Q(S_t,A_t)} \leftarrow \val{Q(S_t,A_t)} + \frac{W}{C(S_t,A_t)}\,\big[\,\rew{G} - \val{Q(S_t,A_t)}\,\big] \qquad W \leftarrow \frac{W}{\pol{b(A_t \mid S_t)}}$$

Walking back through an episode of the behavior policy $\pol{b}$; stop at the first action that is not greedy.

### One change from on-policy MC control {#change}

| | episodes come from | values learned for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [[mc-control]] | the ε-greedy policy | the same ε-greedy policy |
| off-policy MC | any soft behavior policy | the greedy policy, via importance weights |

### Pseudocode

::: pseudocode
Parameters: a soft behavior policy $\pol{b}$ (here: random), discount $\gam$
Set $\val{Q(s,a)} = 0$ and $C(s,a) = 0$ for every state and action {#init}
Repeat for each episode:
  Deal or reset: the first state $S_0$ {#start}
  Play the episode to the end with $\pol{b}$ {#generate}
  $\rew{G} \leftarrow 0$ and $W \leftarrow 1$ {#g0}
  For $t = T-1$ down to $0$: $\rew{G} \leftarrow \gam\,\rew{G} + \rew{R_{t+1}}$ {#return}
    $C \leftarrow C + W$ and $\val{Q(S_t,A_t)} \leftarrow \val{Q(S_t,A_t)} + \tfrac{W}{C}\,[\rew{G} - \val{Q(S_t,A_t)}]$ {#update}
    If $A_t$ is not the greedy action in $S_t$, stop: earlier steps say nothing about the greedy policy {#cut}
    $W \leftarrow W / \pol{b(A_t \mid S_t)}$ {#weight}
:::

### Perks

- Learns the optimal policy from exploratory, or even random, behavior. [See it](lab:blackjack-control)
- Can reuse data it did not generate, as long as the behavior's probabilities are known.
- The target policy is the greedy one: no exploring cost in what is learned.

### Flaws

- Learns only from the tails of episodes, after the last non-greedy action: slow when episodes are long.
- Importance weights can be large and erratic: one lucky episode can mislead an estimate for a while.
- Needs the behavior policy's probabilities, and coverage of every action the target might take.

### Knobs

| Knob | Too low | Too high |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\eps$ of an ε-greedy behavior | little exploration beyond the greedy path | most episodes cut short, little to learn from |
| episodes | strategy still rough | (none: more only helps) |

### Pitfalls

- Breaking ties at random in the greedy policy: the test “was this the greedy action?” then flips at random.
- Forgetting that the last action needs no ratio: its return is used with weight 1.
- A behavior policy that never takes some action the target might: coverage fails, and those values are never learned.

### Check yourself {#check}

::: question
Why can the transition probabilities be left out of the importance ratio?
---
They appear in both the numerator and the denominator, once per step, and cancel. Only the two policies' probabilities remain, so no model is needed.
:::

::: question
Why does the backward walk stop at the first non-greedy action?
---
The greedy target policy would never have taken it, so the importance ratio of every earlier step is 0: what came before does not describe the greedy policy.
:::

::: question
Learning from a random player, which states are learned slowest?
---
States early in long episodes: for their returns to count, every later random move must happen to agree with the greedy policy, which becomes exponentially unlikely with the number of moves.
:::
