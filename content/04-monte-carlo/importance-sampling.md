## Textbook

### Expectations under the wrong distribution {#problem}

Importance sampling is a general technique for estimating expected values under one distribution given samples from another. Let $X$ be drawn from a distribution $b$, and suppose we want the expectation of $f(X)$ under a different distribution $\pi$, which gives positive probability only where $b$ does. Then

$$\mathbb{E}_\pi\big[f(X)\big] = \sum_x \pi(x)\, f(x) = \sum_x b(x)\,\frac{\pi(x)}{b(x)}\, f(x) = \mathbb{E}_b\Big[\frac{\pi(X)}{b(X)}\, f(X)\Big]. \label{identity}$$

Each sample is weighted by the **importance ratio** $\pi(x)/b(x)$: samples that are more likely under $\pi$ than under $b$ count more, those that are less likely count less. In off-policy learning, the samples are episodes generated by a behavior policy $\pol{b}$, and the expectation wanted is a value under a target policy $\pol{\pi}$ ([[off-policy-mc]]).

### The ratio of a trajectory {#ratio}

Starting in $S_t$, the probability of the trajectory $A_t, S_{t+1}, A_{t+1}, \ldots, S_T$ under any policy is a product of policy probabilities and transition probabilities. In the ratio the transition probabilities cancel ([[off-policy-mc]]):

$$\rho_{t:T-1} = \prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \frac{\pol{\pi(A_k \mid S_k)}}{\pol{b(A_k \mid S_k)}}. \label{traj-ratio}$$

::: theorem {#thm-unbiased} The weighted return is unbiased
If $\pol{\pi(a \mid s)} > 0$ implies $\pol{b(a \mid s)} > 0$ (coverage), then $\mathbb{E}_b\big[\rho_{t:T-1}\,\rew{G_t} \mid S_t = s\big] = \val{v_\pi(s)}$.
:::

::: proof
Apply \ref{identity} with $X$ the whole trajectory after $S_t = s$ and $f(X) = G_t$. The ratio of the trajectory's probabilities under $\pol{\pi}$ and $\pol{b}$ is \ref{traj-ratio}, since the dynamics $p$ are the same under both and cancel. The expectation of $G_t$ under $\pol{\pi}$ is $v_\pi(s)$ by definition.
:::

### Ordinary and weighted importance sampling {#estimators}

Let $\mathcal{T}(s)$ be the set of time steps at which $s$ is visited, across all episodes (first visits only, for first-visit methods), and $T(t)$ the end of the episode containing $t$. **Ordinary importance sampling** averages the weighted returns:

$$\val{V(s)} = \frac{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}(s)} \rho_{t:T(t)-1}\,\rew{G_t}}{|\mathcal{T}(s)|}. \label{ordinary}$$

**Weighted importance sampling** divides by the sum of the weights instead (and is 0 when they sum to 0):

$$\val{V(s)} = \frac{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}(s)} \rho_{t:T(t)-1}\,\rew{G_t}}{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}(s)} \rho_{t:T(t)-1}}. \label{weighted}$$

The difference shows most clearly with a single return. In the weighted estimate, the ratio cancels: the estimate is the observed return, whatever its weight. That is a sample of $\val{v_b(s)}$, not of $\val{v_\pi(s)}$, so the weighted estimator is **biased**, though the bias vanishes as more returns are averaged. The ordinary estimate is $\rho\,\rew{G}$: unbiased in expectation, but if the ratio is 10, the estimate is ten times the observed return, which may be far from any value the target policy could have.

| | ordinary | weighted |
| --- | --- | --- |
| bias (first-visit) | none | yes, vanishing as returns accumulate |
| variance | can be unbounded, since ratios can be | bounded: no single return weighs more than 1 |
| in practice | rarely used alone | strongly preferred |

Every-visit versions of both estimators are biased, with bias that falls to zero as the number of samples grows. Both have incremental implementations: the weighted one keeps a running sum of the weights for each state, $C \leftarrow C + W$ and $V \leftarrow V + \tfrac{W}{C}(G - V)$ ([[off-policy-mc]]).

### Example: one Blackjack state {#example}

::: example {#ex-bj} Off-policy estimation of a Blackjack state (Sutton & Barto, Example 5.4)
Estimate the value of a single Blackjack state, a player's sum of 13 with a usable ace against a dealer showing 2, under the target policy that sticks only on 20 or 21 ([[mc-prediction]]). The behavior policy hits or sticks at random with equal probability. Every episode starts in this state. Since the target policy is deterministic, each ratio is $2^k$ if the behavior made the target's choice at all $k$ decisions of the episode, and 0 otherwise. The true value, computed exactly from the rules, is $-0.27720$.
:::

Both errors approach zero as episodes accumulate (\ref{fig-bj}), but the weighted estimator is much better at first. That is the typical pattern: the variance of ordinary importance sampling dominates its advantage of being unbiased.

### Infinite variance {#infinite}

The variance of ordinary importance sampling is typically infinite whenever the ratios are unbounded, and that happens easily with loops in a trajectory.

::: example {#ex-loop} Infinite variance (Sutton & Barto, Example 5.5)
A single nonterminal state $s$ has two actions. *Right* ends the episode with reward 0. *Left* returns to $s$ with probability 0.9 and ends the episode with reward $+1$ with probability 0.1. The target policy always goes left, so $\val{v_\pi(s)} = 1$. The behavior policy goes left or right with equal probability, and $\gam = 1$.
:::

Only episodes in which the behavior goes left every time have a nonzero ratio. An episode that goes left $k + 1$ times and then ends has ratio $2^{k+1}$ and return 1, and probability $(\tfrac12 \cdot 0.9)^k\,(\tfrac12 \cdot 0.1)$ under the behavior. The second moment of the weighted return is therefore

$$\mathbb{E}_b\big[(\rho\,\rew{G})^2\big] = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \big(\tfrac12 \cdot 0.9\big)^k \big(\tfrac12 \cdot 0.1\big)\, 4^{k+1} = 0.2 \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} 1.8^k = \infty. \label{moment}$$

Its mean is 1, so the ordinary estimate is unbiased, but its variance is infinite. In practice the estimates sit near or below 1 for long stretches and then jump when a rare long episode with a huge ratio arrives, and they fail to converge in any useful sense even after millions of episodes (\ref{fig-loop}). Weighted importance sampling would give exactly 1 after the first episode that ends with the left action.

:::

### Reducing variance {#per-decision}

The return is a sum of rewards, and each reward needs only the ratios of the actions taken *before* it. The factors for later actions have expectation 1 and contribute only variance:

$$\mathbb{E}_b\big[\rho_{t:T-1}\,\rew{R_{t+k+1}}\big] = \mathbb{E}_b\big[\rho_{t:t+k}\,\rew{R_{t+k+1}}\big]. \label{per-decision-eq}$$

Weighting each reward by its own truncated ratio gives **per-decision importance sampling**, which has the same expectation as the ordinary estimator and lower variance (Precup, Sutton & Singh, 2000). With discounting, a related idea treats the discount as a probability of termination, so that distant rewards need fewer ratios. These ideas carry over to multi-step temporal-difference methods and eligibility traces ([[n-step-td]], [[td-lambda]]).

### Where importance ratios appear later {#later}

- **One-step Q-learning needs none.** Its target uses the greedy action's value directly, and no action after the one being evaluated is sampled ([[q-learning]]).
- **Expected SARSA needs none.** It averages over the target policy's next actions instead of sampling them ([[expected-sarsa]]).
- **Multi-step off-policy methods need them,** one ratio per step of lookahead, with the variance problems above.
- **Policy-gradient methods reuse experience with them.** PPO maximizes an objective weighted by $\pol{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)} / \pol{\pi_{\text{old}}(a \mid s)}$, and clips the ratio to keep its variance in check ([[ppo]]; Schulman et al., 2017).

### Historical remarks {#history}

Importance sampling is a classical variance-reduction and change-of-measure technique of Monte Carlo simulation (Kahn & Marshall, 1953). Weighted importance sampling and its properties are discussed by Hesterberg (1988). Precup, Sutton and Singh (2000) brought importance sampling into off-policy temporal-difference learning, including per-decision importance sampling; Mahmood, van Hasselt and Sutton (2014) extended weighted importance sampling to linear function approximation. The two examples are from Sutton and Barto (§5.5).

## Card

### Idea

To learn about policy π from episodes of policy b, weight each return by the ratio of the probabilities the two policies give its actions. **Ordinary** importance sampling averages the weighted returns: unbiased, but its variance can be enormous. **Weighted** importance sampling divides by the sum of the weights: slightly biased, much steadier.

::: analogy
Estimating the average height of a town's adults from a survey taken at a basketball court. Each tall person interviewed should count for less, each short one for more, in proportion to how over- or under-represented they are at the court compared with the town.
:::

### In symbols {#formula}

$$\rho_{t:T-1} = \prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \frac{\pol{\pi(A_k \mid S_k)}}{\pol{b(A_k \mid S_k)}} \qquad \text{ordinary: } \frac{\sum \rho\,\rew{G}}{n} \qquad \text{weighted: } \frac{\sum \rho\,\rew{G}}{\sum \rho}$$

Unbiased: $\mathbb{E}_b[\rho_{t:T-1}\,\rew{G_t} \mid S_t = s] = \val{v_\pi(s)}$, provided $\pol{b}$ covers $\pol{\pi}$.

### Why it matters {#why}

It is what makes off-policy learning from returns possible: learning the best policy while behaving otherwise, or reusing old data. Its variance is the main obstacle, and many later methods are designed to avoid or tame it. [See it](lab:blackjack-control)

### Pitfalls

- No coverage: if the behavior never takes an action the target might, no amount of weighting recovers it.
- Ordinary importance sampling with long episodes: ratios multiply, and the variance can be infinite.
- Forgetting that the transition probabilities cancel: no model is needed to compute the ratio.

### Check yourself {#check}

::: question
With a single return, what are the weighted and the ordinary estimates?
---
Weighted: the return itself, since the ratio cancels. Ordinary: the ratio times the return.
:::

::: question
The target policy is deterministic and the behavior picks between two actions at random. What are the possible ratios of a 3-step episode?
---
$2^3 = 8$ if the behavior chose the target's action at all three steps, and 0 otherwise.
:::

::: question
Why can the variance of ordinary importance sampling be infinite?
---
Ratios are products over the steps of an episode, so they grow exponentially with its length. If long episodes are likely enough, the second moment of the weighted return is an infinite sum, as in the one-state example.
:::
